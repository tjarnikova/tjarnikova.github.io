2026-09 | Let things shake



Everyone keeps talking about the robots. On the morning of September 9, I told a fluid dynamicist that the Navier-Stokes Millenium Prize was solved and he said Is That A Joke and pulled out his phone to check the BBC and there it was, a headline no one peripheral to math expected to see in their lifetime. It was solved, of course, by one of the Big Robots. (As far as anyone can tell, the owners of the Big Robot figured out in what direction some human mathematicians had made progress and then threw several million dollars’ worth of compute that way, then attempted to bar one of the original mathematicians from credit because he worked at the other Big Robot company.)

Some of my work is programming and no one really ever taught me how, so using the Big Robots can make some aspects of my job more fun. I can try more things, and I spend less time trying to figure out why the model is outputting WARNING A PROCESS REFUSED TO DIE*. Simultaneously I’m nervous, like everyone, about the obvious problems: misalignment, the hoarding of huge amounts of power by very few people, the weird technofascism of silicon valley, useful things to know buried in piles of credible-sounding mess, etc. About outsourcing thinking at scale. I guess as usual, it makes sense to figure out why you want to do something before trying to do it as fast as possible. The risk I took was calculated but it turns out I’m bad at math.
*a real error message. girl, same.**
**I used to find, and still do find, a lot of joy (& meaning) in these moments of humanity in the command line. (often expressed as frustration: DELETE THIS LINE AND YOU WILL GET WHAT YOU DESERVE.) We now build a synthetic intelligence on top of the Linux nerds. Fine. But know what it is you’re doing, and why.


Everyone has opinions about the robots; I’m really enjoying Meghan O’Geiblyn’s. O’Geiblyn’s writing reflects her life trajectory - raised fundamentalist, lost faith at seminary, became interested in computing and consciousness. Human God Animal Machine is about all of that, from the perspective of someone who has thought hard and changed their mind a few times. I found it thoughtful and rigorous, but surprisingly approachable for someone (me) who has only very fuzzy knowledge of most of the above.

I found Meghan O’Geiblyn through this essay (Simone Weil mentioned, unsurprisingly.), recommended by Molly Young. Molly Young used to recommend books at the NYT and now does any number of interesting things. she’s one of those rare people where I’ll read any piece of her reporting just because it’s her writing it: there’s a very pleasing internal grammar to what she notices and how she talks about it. This was one of the most singular things I’ve ever read about pregnancy and brith.

For when reading many words is hard: Short form joys can be so nice. This weather report, this surf report (same guy, gosh!), these personals.

(On the spectrum of “here’s a wholesome place to buy used books on the World Wide Web” to “grief is nonlinear”, I’m tending more towards the former, but all welcome. Do you have one? Tell me!
